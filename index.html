How twin-container temporary immersion systems work

Two containers joined by a tube: one holds the plants, the other stores the medium. Air pressure or a lift moves the medium across to immerse the explants, then it returns to the storage container for the aeration period.

Twin containers at a glance

One of the oldest TIS types (Lyam et al., 2012; M. Paula, 2012), and still one of the most used.

24%of published TIS papers report a twin-flask design (De Carlo et al., 2021)
~5 kPalow-pressure sterile air needed when the containers are linked from the top
2ways to link them: from the top, or from the bottom (same or different levels)

Advantages

  • Simple and sterileTwin-flask TISs are easy to operate and stay sterile during cultivation (Arencibia et al., 2013).
  • Flexible materialsBottles, jars or flasks of glass, plastic or polymer, linked by a glass or plastic pipe or a silicone tube (Schumann et al., 2012; Yang et al., 2010).
  • Partial or total transferWith a bottom connection you choose how much medium moves; knowing the containers' dimensions and volume is needed to set this.
  • Pneumatic lift saves energyThe cylinder works only during immersion, which is much shorter than aeration, so energy use is lower and the solenoid valve carries current for less time, extending its life.

Disadvantages

  • Poor ventilationIf air does not flow from the storage container to the culture container at the end of aeration, the culture headspace is poorly renewed (Georgiev et al., 2014).
  • No CO₂ enrichmentThe same condition also means no CO₂ enrichment of the culture container (Georgiev et al., 2014). Untick the flush option above to see it.
  • Static pressure loss (top link)With a top connection the air must overcome the static head of the liquid column, about 5 kPa.
